# Effort Tracking and Estimation

---

# Effort Estimation

### How I Estimated Effort
I have roughly four years of software development experience, primarily in hobby and academic settings rather than professional environments. Over that time, I have seen many common development scenarios play out repeatedly, particularly how bugs, flawed logic, and unforeseen issues can significantly extend timelines. Because of this, I have grown accustomed to planning a 50% time buffer for every major part of a coding project.

I applied this same approach throughout the course when estimating homework assignments. Early on, I was somewhat overconfident in my ability to complete assignments quickly. However, as I calibrated to the actual difficulty and complexity of the coursework, I became much more accurate in estimating how long tasks would take, even while still leaving a 50% buffer.

Going into the final project, my estimates were generally not far off. However, because the range of issues I worked on was very broad, I could not reliably reference past issues to estimate future ones. Instead, I relied more heavily on intuition and general subject-matter knowledge (e.g., algorithms, data structures, and system design) when estimating effort.
 
### Did Estimating in Advance Provide Benefits?
Estimating in advance did not provide much benefit for me in this particular project. From the very beginning, I recognized that the project scope was extremely ambitious for a three-week timeline. As a result, my strategy was to spend as much time as possible early in the project to get critical work done.

One major unknown was how effectively my group members would be able to complete their assigned issues. Because of this uncertainty, I focused on taking control of the parts I could directly influence and moved forward as aggressively as possible. My goal was to complete as many issues as I could, as early as I could, rather than pacing work based on estimates.

This approach is reflected in my commit history, which shows large bursts of activity followed by periods of inactivity when I was unable to work on the project. Because I would often work through many issues consecutively in long sessions—regardless of their complexity—formal estimation did not meaningfully influence my workflow or decision-making.

---

# Effort Tracking

### Was Tracking Actual Effort Useful?
Tracking effort was somewhat useful in hindsight. Reviewing my logged effort helped highlight that I am not as efficient as I should be when working on relatively simple coding tasks. These tasks should ideally take very little time, and spending excessive time on them is a weakness—especially in the context of technical interviews, where getting stuck on basic problems can significantly hurt performance.

While effort tracking did not strongly influence decisions during the project itself, it did provide insight into areas where I need to improve my speed and efficiency.

### How Did You Track Actual Effort?
My effort tracking was not very accurate. I did not use any dedicated tools or timers. Instead, I would loosely note the time when I started working on an issue and then check the clock, commit time, or merge time when I finished.

When logging effort, I relied largely on memory to approximate how long each task took. Because of this, the recorded effort should be considered a rough estimate rather than a precise measurement.

---

# Reflection

### What Would You Change Next Time?
If I were to do this again, I would group issues more aggressively. Breaking issues down further would likely increase the amount of time lost to estimation and tracking overhead. In practice, that overhead competes directly with time that could be spent making actual progress.

Rather than estimating and logging many small tasks, I believe working on fewer, broader units of work would be more efficient. Minimizing logging overhead would allow me to focus more on completing tasks rather than documenting them.
